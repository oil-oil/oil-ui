# First-screen skeletons for persuasive pages

Landing pages, brand pages, product launch pages, exhibition pages, and other pages whose main job is to persuade or convey an idea choose their first-screen skeleton by this guide (the overall design direction process is in [Design direction](design-direction.md)).

The model's most stubborn habit is the split layout: a big title on one side, and a description, a button, or an image on the other. Changing the typeface, the palette, or the material does not shift it, because it was decided before any copy was written: think of a title, a paragraph, and a button, lay them out, and two columns follow naturally. So the skeleton must be decided before any copy is written.

Each direction first picks one first-screen skeleton from the table below, then draws the first-screen wireframe with 3–5 boxes: what each block is, how much of the first screen it takes, and where reading starts.

| Skeleton | What the first screen looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Full-bleed image with text overlay | One image fills the first screen, with the title and action placed inside the image's safe area |
| A single centered object | One object or one sentence sits in the center, surrounded by large areas of empty space |
| The screen is the interface | The first screen is the product interface or a physical object itself, and the title, navigation, and buttons grow on it |
| Full-bleed type | The text itself is the picture, large enough to fill the first screen, run past the edges, or be cropped |
| Full-width stack | A full-width headline spans the entire width, with content directly beneath it |
| Grid collage | Several pieces of content appear at once, arranged on a grid or as a collage, with no single protagonist |
| Vertical or diagonal | Text set vertically, cut on a slant, or rotated, breaking left-to-right reading |
| Split layout | Text on one side, visual on the other. This is the model's default. At most one direction per round may use it, and it must state why nothing else would do. The other side must be an image, color block, or graphic device that crosses the structure, not a product interface framed in a card |

Rules:
- Directions in the same round use different skeletons.
- A so-called "nine-column headline, three-column description" also counts as a split layout.
- Write copy only after the skeleton is set. The copy adapts to the skeleton. Do not let the skeleton adapt to the "title + description + button" combination.

How bold the composition is has nothing to do with tone. A calm, polished product can just as well put one sentence in the center of an empty first screen, run a vertical headline through the whole screen, or let the interface itself fill the first screen. Tone decides the volume. The skeleton decides the structure. Do not fall back to the split layout because the tone leans calm.

Every round has at least one direction that feels a little risky to you: it would look out of place among its peers, yet it still looks good and works completely. The model only does this when asked explicitly. If what you are thinking is "this can't possibly work", the direction is usually right, and the result is often better than expected.

The core of the most common template is not the split layout itself. It is "title and two buttons on the left, a product interface in a rounded card on the right". That card is almost always the thing to delete: either let the product interface itself fill the first screen, or replace it with an image, a color block, or a graphic device that crosses the layout structure.

## Scroll narrative

A persuasive page is more than its first screen. Once the skeleton is set, plan the scroll motion by [Scroll narrative](scroll-narrative.md): sections enter according to their content, plus at least one scroll-driven narrative, with the technique chosen from this page's key visual and written into the "Motion" field of the direction card. They are checked together with the three basic motions.
